Most people searching for a nsocks alternative aren't annoyed at residential proxies in general. They're annoyed at one specific thing about how NSOCKS sells them — the unit, the clock, the API, or the lack of a trial. Fix that one thing and the hunt usually ends.

## The four reasons people start looking

**The unit mismatch.** NSOCKS runs two product lines that are priced in different currencies of the proxy world. Rotating residential is billed per gigabyte. The per-IP SOCKS5 shop is billed per address. If you're comparing a $/GB headline against a $/IP headline, you aren't comparing anything yet — you're comparing a car's price per kilometre against its price per year.

**The expiry clock.** Published reviews of NSOCKS's rotating residential packs put validity at around 30 days on the entry tiers. Burn a 10 GB pack in a week and the effective rate is fine. Use half of it over two months and your real cost per gigabyte roughly doubles. That's not a scam, it's just a pricing model that assumes you consume on schedule.

**The API is client-side.** You have to whitelist your device IP first, then generate proxy lists from the dashboard. That works for personal automation. There's no public reseller or white-label program, so if you're running a stack for clients, volume terms come from a conversation with support, not a signup form.

**No self-service trial on the bandwidth products.** Reviews of the service note that traffic products start with a paid pack. You can spend $5 to find out, but you can't find out for free.

None of that makes NSOCKS a bad provider — it's a real pool with real volume pricing, and for a high-consumption operation burning traffic daily it can price out well. It's specifically the irregular user who pays for the mismatch.

## Four numbers to write down before you switch

Whatever provider you land on, copy these down off their own pages. They decide more than the headline rate:

1. **The rate with no commitment** — the smallest pack, not the 3,000 GB tier.
2. **The smallest purchase allowed** — can you start at $15, or do you have to commit to a hundred dollars?
3. **What happens to unspent balance** — expires, rolls over, or never expires.
4. **What you're billed for** — IPs held, or bytes moved.

A provider that wins on number one can lose badly on number three. That's the whole game.

## IP-based, GB-based, or bundle?

The wrong model costs you money faster than the wrong provider.

**Pick IP-based if** each of your sessions needs to look like one consistent person for hours. Account management, logged-in scraping, carts, anything where switching IPs mid-flow triggers a challenge. You get unlimited traffic during the IP's active window, which is the real appeal — bandwidth-heavy work stops being a cost decision. The trade-off is operational: this model runs through the desktop app, so it's a workstation setup rather than something you spin up from a browser tab.

**Pick GB-based if** your traffic is bursty or your rotation is aggressive. Generate unlimited endpoints, pay only for bytes moved, and choose rotating mode (new IP per request) or sticky mode (same IP until the session timer ends). This is the closer match for SERP checks, price monitoring, ad verification and lightweight automation, where requests are small and IP variety is the point.

**Pick a bundle if** you're running both at once — some long-lived sessions, some high-rotation work — and you'd rather buy one pack per client or project than reconcile two balances.

## Making the switch without wasting money

A short checklist that keeps the migration cheap:

1. **Buy the smallest pack on the new provider first.** For 9Proxy that's the $15 GB pack or the $24 IP pack. Run your actual target list, not a generic speed test.
2. **Measure cost per successful response, not per request.** Divide spend by responses that came back clean. This reorders most proxy price comparisons the moment you do it.
3. **Test the failure modes you actually care about** — block pages, CAPTCHA rates, login persistence on sticky sessions.
4. **Check your concurrency limits before you commit to a big tier.** On real workloads, concurrent connections and requests per second bind long before bandwidth does, and those numbers usually live in the docs rather than on the pricing page.
5. **Only then buy volume.** The per-unit discount is worthless if the IPs don't work on your targets.
